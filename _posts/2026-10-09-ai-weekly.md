## 1. Mistral ships Large 4 ("Le Chonk") in public preview

On October 6, Mistral AI released **Mistral Large 4** — nicknamed "Le Chonk" internally — as a public API preview. It is a granular mixture-of-experts model with **1.05 trillion total parameters** and roughly 49 billion active per token (Mistral's own pages disagree slightly — the model card says 52B, the announcement says 49B), plus a 1.6B-parameter vision encoder for native image input and a **1-million-token context window**.

The training story is part of the product: trained from scratch on **3,800 NVIDIA Grace Blackwell GPUs in Mistral's own European datacenters**, covering 160+ languages including every official EU language. The API is live now at $1.36/$4.18 per million input/output tokens (preview pricing), but the **open weights don't land until October 27** — so nobody can self-host it yet.

Mistral's pitch is cybersecurity. The company claims 93% on Cybench and 82% on a vulnerability reproduce-and-patch task — and says leading closed models score near zero on the same tasks because provider-level refusals block them. The argument: defenders doing legitimate vulnerability research shouldn't need permission from a closed-model provider.

### Why it matters

Two stories in one: Europe's largest model built entirely on European infrastructure, and the first serious attempt to market refusal policies as a competitive feature gap rather than a safety virtue. The spec discrepancy (49B vs 52B active) is a small reminder to read the model card, not the announcement.

## 2. OpenAI dumps 719 math manuscripts; mathematicians push back

Also on October 6, OpenAI published **719 manuscripts** (722 initially, three withdrawn over a sign error) organized into 372 topic families on its public [math repository](https://github.com/openai/math). All were produced by an **internal, unreleased frontier model** that attempted roughly 4,000 open problems, averaging about three hours of ChatGPT Pro-equivalent compute per result. Only around 42% of top-line results carry Lean formalizations; the rest are unverified.

The release comes one week after the Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence (at the Institute for Advanced Study) asked labs to **stop testing advanced problems on proprietary models** the scientific community can't access. The group said its advisory role was not an endorsement of how OpenAI obtained the results. Meanwhile, mathematicians — including researchers connected to Terence Tao — are combing the batch for gaps, duplication, and already-solved problems.

The contrast with Anthropic is sharp. On October 5, Anthropic published one tightly scoped result — raising the Riemann zeta lower bound from 41.6% to 67.2% — with a full methodology writeup and Lean formalization. Volume and speed versus defensibility.

### Why it matters

The tally is the least interesting part. What matters is the verification queue: publication is not proof, and independent human checking will take months. OpenAI is betting quantity eventually becomes recognized contribution; the mathematical community is deciding whether that's how it works.

## 3. OpenAI's revenue miss rattles the AI trade

On October 8, the *Financial Times* reported that OpenAI told investors its **annualized revenue is nearing $50 billion** — about **$20 billion less** than the $70 billion figure previously reported in the press. A source familiar with the documents said the $70B figure didn't come from OpenAI; it likely reflected net-vs-gross accounting differences with Anthropic's reported numbers.

Markets noticed. The **Nasdaq sank 1.25%** on Thursday, its worst day since mid-August; the S&P 500 fell 0.5%. Nvidia dropped 2.9%, Intel 5.3%, Oracle 5.5%. OpenAI declined to comment.

### Why it matters

The AI trade runs on one conviction: demand growth justifies the buildout spend. A $20B gap between the rumor and the number doesn't invalidate that conviction, but it re-prices it — and the sell-off showed how far AI valuations now travel on investor math rather than cash flow.
