On September 30, Google DeepMind announced **Gemini 4 Argon**, its first flagship frontier model in more than seven months.

Headline specs: an industry-leading **1-million-token output limit** (up from 64K), tuned for long-horizon work — real-world software engineering, enterprise knowledge work (legal, finance), and cybersecurity defense. Introductory pricing is $2 input / $10 output per million tokens.

Google's claimed results: ties for first on CWE-bench v1 (68%), a new state of the art on DeepSWE v1.1 (77.9%), and Wiz reportedly used it to find a critical flaw in widely-used healthcare software that earlier frontier models missed. Artificial Analysis scores it around 53 on its Intelligence Index — roughly tied with OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, just behind Claude's 5.5 models. (All benchmarks are Google-reported; independent tables weren't published at launch, so treat the margins cautiously.)

The rollout is the real story: initial access goes **only to vetted trusted cyber defenders through the Fairwind Program** — including a version "without cyber guardrails" — while Google participates in the US government's voluntary pre-release access process. Paid API customers and AI Ultra subscribers come next; no general date announced. Thousands of Google employees already use it internally.

### Why it matters

A Google flagship always resets the frontier conversation — but the "defenders first" gating is itself the news. Capabilities have crossed a threshold where the company treats cyber-offense potential as a release-management constraint. It sharpens the asymmetry of the AI security landscape: defenders get the tool before the public does.
