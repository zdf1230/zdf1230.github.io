## 1. Google's Gemini 4 Carbon: its strongest model, but only for cyber defenders — for now

On October 28, Google DeepMind announced **Gemini 4 Carbon**, two weeks after Gemini 4 Argon. Google calls it "our strongest model with state-of-the-art cybersecurity and long horizon capabilities." The numbers it leads with are security numbers: **88.2% on SEC-bench Pro** for finding real vulnerabilities, **54.8% on ExploitGym** for validating them through exploitation, and **71.0% on CWE-bench** for patching them. Google notes those security scores were measured without guardrails. On internal tests, Carbon more than doubled Argon's success rate at generating secure code, and Google says its teams have seen it work for days on complex engineering problems.

Carbon also took the top spot on the newly revised Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v5, scoring **59**, one point ahead of Claude Opus 5.5 at 58.

The catch is access. Carbon goes first to "a subset of cyber defenders" through Google's Fairwind Program, widening gradually to trusted partners. Google has set no date for developers, businesses, or consumers, and no general price beyond the introductory rate it says will match Argon's. In other words, the week's top-ranked model is, for most people, a press release with a waitlist attached.

### Why it matters

This is a different kind of frontier launch: capability framed as defence, not as a chatbot upgrade. A model that finds, validates, and patches flaws in one loop is genuinely useful to security teams — and, in the wrong hands, the same loop is an offensive tool. Google's staged rollout is the honest response to that dual use. The question to watch is whether "defenders first" stays a sequencing choice or becomes the template for how the most capable models are distributed.

## 2. Anthropic files its S-1: $16.3B in six months, and a very large asterisk

On October 26, Anthropic publicly filed its Form S-1 for a proposed Nasdaq listing of Class A shares under the ticker **ANT**. The share count and price range are blank, the registration is not yet effective, and no shares can be sold until the SEC signs off. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley are listed first among the underwriters. Reports put a possible listing in the week of November 16, potentially raising $100 billion or more at a $1.5–2 trillion valuation — those figures are press reports, not in the filing.

The filing gives public investors their first audited look at a frontier lab's finances, and the headline numbers are extreme in both directions. Revenue for the six months ended June 30, 2026 was **$16.286 billion**, up from $1.163 billion a year earlier and more than triple all of 2025 ($4.585 billion). The second quarter alone produced $11.6 billion in revenue and $974 million in GAAP operating income. The half-year operating loss narrowed to $693 million.

Then the asterisk: a **$80.748 billion net loss** for the same six months. About $72.4 billion of it is a non-cash revaluation of convertible notes, derivative liabilities, and SAFEs as Anthropic's valuation rose — accounting, not cash burn. Anthropic held $131.651 billion in cash, cash equivalents, and short-term investments at June 30. Usage-based fees made up 80% of first-half revenue, subscriptions the rest.

### Why it matters

This is the first major AI lab to file publicly, and it reframes the funding debate with real numbers. The growth is real and steep; so is the compute bill behind it. A public listing will force the question the private market has deferred: whether a company can keep growing revenue 14x year over year while its largest cost — compute — grows nearly as fast. Watch the amended filings for price, size, and any Q3 numbers before treating the reported valuation as a fact.

## 3. OpenAI's GPT-6.1 Terra: computer use at half the price

Also on October 26, OpenAI released **GPT-6.1 Terra**, its mid-tier model, replacing GPT-5.6 Terra. It is built for the mundane work that fills an office day: drafting proposals, reading technical drawings, filling out forms, editing video, reading invoices and entering them into accounting software. It is available in ChatGPT Work and Codex for Plus, Pro, Enterprise, and Edu users, and through the API as `gpt-6.1-terra`.

The price is the story: **$1 per million input tokens, $0.05 cached input, $5 per million output** — half the price of GPT-5.6 Terra and GPT-6.1 Sol. OpenAI's own benchmarks claim the model scores 15 percentage points higher than its predecessor on Agents' Last Exam at medium effort, at about a third of the cost, and matches GPT-6 Astra on that benchmark at a tenth of the price per token, completing tasks more than three times faster. On Terminal-Bench 4.0 it solves 50.3% of tasks at maximum effort, up from 21.5%. Its low-effort factual error rate fell from 53.6% to 16.7%, per OpenAI. All of these are vendor numbers; independent tests are not out yet.

### Why it matters

Frontier capability keeps getting cheaper, but Terra is a different kind of price cut: it targets the tier where agents actually do volume work. If the cost-per-task claims hold up even roughly, computer-use automation stops being a demo budget item and becomes something a team can run all day. That moves the bottleneck from model price to controls — what the agent is allowed to click, submit, and pay for — which is exactly where this week's oversight letters (see below) are pointing.
